## Part 2 — Hollins Wood Nature Reserve (UK male guide)

Welcome to Hollins Wood. Before we set off, let me tell you a little about the reserve. People often assume it became a reserve because of the rare birds that nest here, and we do have some, but in fact it was protected because local people raised the money to buy the land when it came up for sale in 1985. **(Q11 B)**

You may have noticed that the main entrance has moved. The old car park by the river was flooded twice in one winter, so we built a new one up the hill. **(Q12 B)** The road is no wider than before, so coaches still need to book.

We have three trails. The red trail is flat and has a hard surface, so it's suitable for wheelchairs and pushchairs. **(Q13 A)** The blue trail is longer and quieter, and it can get muddy, but we only close a trail if a tree has fallen.

A few requests. Dogs are welcome on the trails, though not in the bird hides. Cycling is fine on the red trail. What we really ask is that you don't feed the ducks on the lake — bread makes them ill and it pollutes the water. **(Q14 B)**

We rely on volunteers, and we have plenty on Saturdays, but on Sunday mornings we are often short of people to help in the shop and on the gate. **(Q15 C)**

Now, a quick guide to what you'll find. At the visitor centre you can borrow binoculars free of charge — just leave a phone number. **(Q16 D)** Films about the wood used to be shown there, but that stopped last year. Down at the lake you can hire a rowing boat by the hour. **(Q17 A)** The old mill no longer grinds flour, but inside there's a display of old tools used by the woodcutters. **(Q18 F)** If you have a picnic in the meadow, that's also where our guided walks start, every day at two. **(Q19 B)** And at the north gate there's a box where you can collect a trail map. **(Q20 G)** Plants are sold at the garden centre next door, not by us.

## Part 4 — The development of lighthouse lights (UK female lecturer)

Today I'm going to talk about how lighthouse lights developed. The earliest lights were simply fires, burning coal or wood, on cliffs or hilltops. **(Q31 wood)** They had an obvious weakness: in bad weather the smoke often hid the light completely from ships at sea. **(Q32 smoke)**

From the 1780s, fires were replaced by lamps that burned oil. **(Q33 oil)** On its own a lamp is not very bright, so curved reflectors were placed behind it. These were made of polished copper, which reflected the light out to sea in a stronger beam. **(Q34 copper)**

The great breakthrough came in 1822, when the French engineer Augustin Fresnel — that's F-R-E-S-N-E-L — designed a new kind of lens. **(Q35 Fresnel)** Instead of one thick, heavy piece, it was made from rings of glass, each one bending the light so that it all travelled in a single beam. **(Q36 glass)** Each lighthouse was also given its own pattern of flashes, so that sailors who saw it could work out their position. **(Q37 position)**

These lenses could weigh several tonnes, so turning them was difficult. The answer was to float the lens on a bath of mercury, which allowed it to rotate smoothly with very little effort. **(Q38 mercury)**

In the twentieth century, electric lamps replaced oil, and eventually automation meant that keepers were no longer needed. The last keepers in Britain left in the 1990s. **(Q39 automation)** Many lighthouses were simply abandoned, but today most of the historic ones have become museums, where visitors can climb to the top. **(Q40 museums)**
